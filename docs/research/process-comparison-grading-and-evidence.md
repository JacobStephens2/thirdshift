# Grading and evidence for a hand-run comparison of two agent processes

Research date: 2026-10-29.

**Question.** A hand-run comparison of two agent processes on ~10 real GitHub issues in a production repository. **Baseline:** one implement session (TDD plus self code-review in-session, Claude), then CI. **Candidate:** a "maker-checker TDD" process — separate agents for test planning, RED tests, GREEN implementation and refactor, each with an independent verifier, plus ≥2-model-family review (Claude + Codex/GPT) over up to five rounds. Each issue is implemented once per process; two blind Graders (one Claude, one Codex/GPT) see the issue and two squashed diffs labelled X/Y in random order; each Arm's tests are cross-run against the other's code. Adoption needs ≥8/10 pair wins, no more correctness failures, ≤2× wall-clock and ≤2× tokens. Asked: (1) judge biases and mitigations; (2) is a sign test on 10 pairs reasonable; (3) evidence on the Candidate's ingredients; (4) confounds, and is one run per Arm enough.

**Method.** Every claim links a primary source (paper, official doc, or this repo's code).

- Read in full by me: Shi et al. (AACL 2025 PDF via `pdftotext`), Crupi et al. ([2507.16587](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16587) HTML).
- **(note)** = number recorded against paper tables in this repo's [`multi-family-review.md`](multi-family-review.md) (2026-10-05, literature to 5 Oct 2026) or [`eval-suite-scaling.md`](eval-suite-scaling.md); I did not re-read those full texts.
- **(agent)** = quoted from arXiv abs/HTML by one of three background agents; I did not re-open those pages. Table rendering was flagged ambiguous on [2604.16790](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16790) and [2604.27727](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.27727) — check those PDFs before quoting in print.
- §2 arithmetic is **my computation**: exact binomial tails plus 200k-draw simulations of thirdshift's own posterior ([`src/verdict.rs`](../../src/verdict.rs), [`src/verdict/levels.rs`](../../src/verdict/levels.rs)). **[unconfirmed]** marks what could not be verified.

## 1. The Graders

**Position bias is the main threat and is worst on near-tied candidates — our case.** MT-Bench measured order consistency on *deliberately near-tied* pairs: GPT-4 **65.0%**, GPT-3.5 46.2%, Claude-v1 23.8%; "Only GPT-4 outputs consistent results in more than 60% of cases" ([2306.05685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685) Table 2, agent). On code, positionally-consistent accuracy exceeds raw accuracy by **8–24%** for frontier judges ([2603.24586](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24586), agent). Shi et al.'s systematic study (15 judges, >100k instances, [AACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.ijcnlp-long.18.pdf)) names our situation: bias "is weakly influenced by prompt length but strongly impacted by the quality gap between solutions"; "those of similar quality are difficult to judge" (§4), and the hardest instances show "closely matched win rates and minimal quality gaps" (§5). Its §C.1 also shows judges agreeing *within* family — the GPT-4 series cluster, and "Claude-3-Opus highly agrees with Claude-3.5-Sonnet" — so one Claude plus one GPT Grader is the right panel.

**Two mitigations, different costs.** Zheng et al. §3.4 is the primary source for our plan — "call a judge twice by swapping the order of two answers", and "If the results are inconsistent after swapping, we can call it a tie" — but at 65% consistency that **abstains on about a third of hard pairs** (agent). Wang et al.'s Balanced Position Calibration instead *aggregates* over orders: GPT-4's agreement with human majority rose 52.7% → 62.5% (κ 0.24 → 0.37), ChatGPT's 44.4% → 58.7%, against 71.7% human-human ([2305.17926](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926) Table 4, agent). Swapping measurably helps and does not close the human gap.

**Pairwise is the right format.** GPT-4's pairwise agreement with humans on non-tie MT-Bench votes was **85%**, "even higher than the agreement among humans (81%)" (§4.2, agent). On code review, SWR-Bench's hit-based pairwise judge reached Cohen's κ **52.8–62.0** against experts, "on par with the inter-human agreement (Kappa range: 56.3 to 62.6)", while a holistic 1–10 scorer managed **31.0–44.9** ([2509.01494](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.01494), agent).

**But reference-free code judgement tracks truth poorly, and grounding is the fix.** GPT-4's Cohen's κ against execution ground truth was **0.21 (Java) / 0.10 (Python)**: it classified 72% of correct implementations right but **misjudged 50% of wrong ones** (Crupi et al., *IEEE TSE*, [2507.16587](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16587) §III-A; it used *absolute* scoring deliberately and ran no swap experiment). Supplying the full problem statement raised judges' ROC-AUC **+0.130 to +0.295** and reshuffled their ranking ([2604.27727](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.27727), agent); reference-free CodeJudge lost Kendall τ 0.612 → 0.502 on HumanEval-X and 0.354 → 0.153 on APPS ([2410.02184](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02184), EMNLP 2024, agent); an execution filter cut GPT-4o false rejections from 54.8% to 16.3% ([doi:10.1007/s10515-026-00638-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10515-026-00638-5), note).

**Self-preference is the weaker worry here; presentation is the stronger one.** Pombal et al. found GPT-5 passing 38.0% of *failing* tests on its own code against 1.9% on other families' (ratio 20.15, cut to 4.18 by a five-family committee) ([2604.06996](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06996), note). But the one peer-reviewed study measuring it on code correctness calls GPT-4's self-bias "minimum" — significant p, negligible effect — and finds a far larger bias **against human-written code** (−0.37, large effect) (Crupi et al., agent); Roytburg et al. attribute 89.6% of measured self-preference to evaluator uncertainty, −89.26% on objective code tasks ([2601.22548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22548), agent). One small preprint measured **style bias (markdown vs prose) at 0.76–0.92 against ≤0.04 for position** ([2604.23178](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23178), n=400, single author — suggestive only, agent), and a "this code is secure" framing cut GPT-4o-mini's vulnerable-file detection from 97.2% to 3.6% ([2603.18740](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18740), note). **[unconfirmed]** No paper quantifies self-preference for *pairwise* code judging, and none tests whether a multi-family jury reduces position bias on code; the jury evidence (PoLL, κ 0.627 → 0.763 over a single GPT-4 judge, [2404.18796](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796), agent) is QA and chat only.

**Grader agreement is weaker evidence than it looks.** Average pairwise error correlation across ten judges was 0.21 — ten carrying the information of ~3.5 independent ones, and present across providers; in up to 28% of comparisons, ignoring shared errors turned a null into a significant win ([2609.22512](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22512)).

## 2. Ten pairs (all numbers my computation)

Exact one-sided binomial tails at p = 0.5: P(X≥8) = 0.0547, P(X≥9) = 0.0107, P(X≥7) = 0.1719. **"≥8 of 10" is a rule with a 5.5% false-adoption rate** — defensible; 7/10 would be 17%. Power is the problem:

| True per-pair win prob | 0.5 | 0.6 | 0.7 | 0.75 | 0.8 | 0.85 | 0.9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P(adopt) | 0.055 | 0.167 | 0.383 | 0.526 | 0.678 | 0.820 | 0.930 |

**A Candidate that genuinely wins 80% of pairs fails this bar 32% of the time.** For 80% power: n = 18 at p = 0.8 (critical 13), n = 37 at p = 0.7 (critical 24).

**thirdshift's Bar is the same test with a dial.** The Bar ([`docs/guide.md`](../guide.md) "Experiments"; [ADR 0025](../adr/0025-a-pass-needs-only-the-margin.md)) puts a Dirichlet with prior 1 over paired per-task difference categories and passes when the 70% lower bound clears the margin; at one trial per task the categories are loss/tie/win — our 10 pairs. Simulated: 8-2 passes (70% interval [+0.292, +0.790]); 7-3 passes ([+0.080, +0.643]); 6-4 inconclusive ([−0.120, +0.482]). At the default margin −0.10 the threshold is **7**/10 (17% false adoption); at margin **+0.20** it is 8/10, matching the sign test row for row. So **"≥8/10" is the Bar at pass level 70% and margin +0.20** — a superiority bar demanding a 0.2 average advantage, not the non-inferiority bar it normally runs. Say so explicitly.

**Ties and voids.** The sign test discards ties, which costs power fast (with 2 ties, 8 pairs need 7 wins for α < 0.05) — reason enough to use the Dirichlet, which treats a tie as a zero-difference category. A **tie** is data and stays in. A **void pair** (crash, rate limit, underspecified issue) is missing data: re-run or drop it **before grading** and record the drop — what the Bar already does (`dropped_as_void`, two fresh retries, [`src/verdict.rs`](../../src/verdict.rs) `validate`). Judging void status after seeing which Arm failed would break the design.

**Reduce to one difference per issue before testing.** Treating 4 Grader verdicts as independent is the clustering error Miller warns about ([2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640) §1.1, §2.2); on real thirdshift data, trial-level pairing moved a p value from 1.00 to 0.25 purely through this (**via** [`eval-suite-scaling.md`](eval-suite-scaling.md)). Pairing is the design's best feature: "paired differences represent a 'free' reduction in estimator variance" (§4.2).

## 3. The Candidate's ingredients

**(a) Separating test authorship from implementation is the best-supported part.** The direct test: "generating tests after faulty code significantly reduces fault detection effectiveness compared to generating tests independently (14% vs. 25%)", per-model reductions 9.1–18.2%, p ≤ 0.009 throughout, with a test-driven workflow beating an agentic one by 7.9–17.7% ([2607.05139](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05139), 5 models, 3 benchmarks, agent). Two warnings in the same paper: elaborate test-planning prompts *hurt* — summarisation, CoT and CoVe cut detection 13.4–15.5% versus plain prompting — and the gap is assertion quality, not test count or coverage. AgentCoder supports the separation and is *cheaper* than rival pipelines: test accuracy 87.8/89.9 against 61.0/51.8 single-agent, at 56.9K/66.3K tokens against MetaGPT's 138.2K/206.5K ([2312.13010](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.13010), self-reported, agent). Guard the failure mode: refining against self-generated tests **increases** overfitting 2.9–3.7 points ([2511.16858](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.16858) Table 1, agent).

**(b) Multi-agent pipelines do not reliably beat simpler ones on repo-level work, and cost several times more.** Agentless resolved **32.00%** of SWE-bench Lite at **$0.70** and 78K tokens per issue; CodeR's multi-agent pipeline **28.33%** at **$3.34** and 324K — **4.8× cost, 4.1× tokens, 3.7 points worse** ([2407.01489](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01489) Table 1, agent). The best-controlled study is Kim et al. ([2512.08296](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08296)): 260 configurations, six benchmarks, three families, compute standardised; effects from +80.8% to −70.0%, "architecture-task alignment determines collaborative success", and **on SWE-bench Verified "all MAS architectures show slight degradation relative to SAS", −2.1% to −14.9%** at several times the tokens (note). MAST found 41–86.7% failure rates across seven multi-agent frameworks, largest intervention +15.6% ([2503.13657](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657), agent). Decisively: at a **matched response budget**, multi-agent debate loses to plain self-consistency, 83.0 vs 88.2 at nine responses ([2310.01798](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798) Table 7, agent) — an unmatched comparison measures spend, not architecture.

**(c) Cross-family review helps the weaker writer and can hurt the stronger one.** All four writer/reviewer orderings on 116 LiveCodeBench problems: GPT-5.5 drafts 71.6% → 89.7% after Claude review and 84.5% after GPT-5.5 review; **Claude drafts 91.4% alone and after Claude self-review, but 82.8% after GPT-5.5 review** (cross- vs same-family for one writer p_BH = .245; Claude self-review beat GPT review of Claude, p_BH = .032) ([2607.21656](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21656), KDD 2026 workshop). SWE-Review agrees strength dominates family: on a strong generator's PRs, cross-family Opus +3.0, same-family +2.8, a weak reviewer **−7.3** ([2607.06065](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06065)) (both note). The one direct cross-family review-ensemble measurement is negative: a second model raised precision 0.486 → 0.627–0.694 but cut recall 0.293 → 0.195–0.226, cross-family no better than same-family ([2606.15689](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15689) Table 9; recall falling under a stated "union" implies an agreement rule, so read it as agreement-gating trading recall for precision). Our Baseline is Claude, the stronger coder in this literature, so the Candidate's GPT reviewer sits in the configuration measured to **hurt**.

**(d) More rounds buy recall at a steep cost in precision, and can damage correct code.** Self-Refine caps its loop at four iterations and quantifies the flattening on code optimisation, 22.0 → 27.0 → 27.9 → 28.8 — **round 1 worth +5.0, rounds 2–3 together +1.8** ([2303.17651](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17651) Fig. 4, agent). CR-Bench: a "find the bugs you missed" loop raised recall 27.0% → 32.8% while signal-to-noise fell 5.11 → 1.95 ([2603.11078](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11078)). Requiring explanations and fixes raised GPT-4o's MBPP false-rejection rate 35.9% → 87.9%, 48.2% of them invented logic errors ([doi:10.1007/s10515-026-00638-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10515-026-00638-5)). Adjudication does not save it: frontier judges caught only 9.23–20.77% of untrustworthy review comments ([2609.37216](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37216), note). And acting on a wrong finding is harmful — staged false accusations in Claude Code damaged correct work in 12.50–60.06% of runs ([2609.32616](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32616), note). Google's deployed reviewer plateaued at a 54% useful-comment ratio, reaching 66% only after suppressing 17 noisy rules ([2405.13565](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.13565), agent). **Five rounds is past where every one of these curves turns.**

**What makes a checker work, and what it costs.** Kamoi et al. (TACL 2024) conclude self-correction succeeds when reliable **external** feedback is available, while intrinsic self-correction "does not improve or even degrade the performance" (survey, no effect sizes of its own; [2406.01297](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01297), agent). Our tests and CI are that feedback; verifiers that only read diffs are the configuration it says fails. On cost, an Opus critic took a Qwen agent from $0.05 to $0.28 per task, and a same-model verifier was cost-neutral only because its prompt-cache hit rate rose 45.6% → 90.8% — which another vendor's reviewer cannot reuse ([2608.08950](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08950), note). **A 2× token ceiling is tight for an eight-stage pipeline plus five review rounds, and may bind before quality does.**

## 4. Variance and confounds

**One run per Arm per issue is not enough, and this is now measured directly.** 60,000 trajectories on SWE-bench Verified, ten runs per configuration: "single-run pass@1 estimates vary by 2.2 to 6.0 percentage points depending on which run is selected", with "standard deviations exceeding 1.5 percentage points even at temperature 0"; "detecting a 2% improvement at p<0.05 with 80% power requires approximately 9 runs per agent under test", 36 runs for 1%; "only 15.5% of tasks (pass^5) are solved consistently across five attempts" ([2602.07150](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07150), agent). A 584-run study on a tabular-ML task found "identical runs of one pairing varied more than the pairings differed from one another", needing "tens to more than a hundred runs of each" ([2609.33812](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33812), agent). METR uses "8 runs per agent/task pair" with a three-level hierarchical bootstrap over families, tasks and attempts ([2503.14499](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499) §2.3, agent). thirdshift's own A/A Experiment, identical Arms, disagreed on 2 of 10 code-review tasks ([`docs/guide.md`](../guide.md), from [#1370](https://github.com/JacobStephens2/thirdshift/issues/1370)) — the same size as the gap between adopt (8/10) and inconclusive (6/10). Temperature 0 does not fix it: 1,000 temperature-0 samples of one prompt gave 80 unique completions, traced to batch-invariance in the kernels ([*Defeating Nondeterminism in LLM Inference*](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/), vendor), and bfloat16 greedy decoding varied accuracy up to 9% from GPU count, type and batch size alone ([2506.09501](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09501), agent).

**But more runs per issue is the wrong purchase.** Miller's Equation 9 gives, for a paired comparison, N = (z_α/2 + z_β)²(Kω² + 2σ²)/δ² trials per Arm — **increasing in K** — so more issues is weakly better than more repeats at every budget, and strictly better when the effect varies by issue ([2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640) §5, **via** [`eval-suite-scaling.md`](eval-suite-scaling.md) §1.3). On thirdshift's code-review data, 3 → 6 trials cut per-task variance 5% for double the tokens; one extra issue cut it 9% for a third of the trials. A few repeats buy **robustness, not precision**: at K = 1 one timeout flips a whole issue.

**Other confounds.**

- **Issue heterogeneity.** With 10 issues one bad issue dominates, so pick and freeze the list before any results. SWE-bench Verified's own screening flagged ~38% of candidates for under-specified statements and 61% for tests that could unfairly fail a valid solution (**via** [`eval-suite-scaling.md`](eval-suite-scaling.md)).
- **Harness, versions, machine and day.** Benchmarks "collapse model, harness, and environment into a single end-to-end score", and harness components can "move the benchmark score by margins comparable to those between adjacent model generations" ([2606.17799](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17799), agent). Pin and record model, tool and base-commit versions, and run both Arms on each issue close together so Arm is not confounded with machine, day or endpoint ([2609.33812](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33812) §3.3, §4.5, agent).
- **Cross-run tests are not a neutral referee.** Each Arm's tests encode its own reading of the issue, so report the two directions separately; a failure is a disagreement to read, not automatically a defect. Weak oracles are real even in curated suites — 7.8% of SWE-bench patches pass validation while failing the developer suite, inflating resolution rates 6.2 points ([2503.15223](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15223), agent).
- **Inseparable ingredients.** The Candidate changes test independence, agent count, verifier presence, model families and round count at once, so a win licenses the bundle, not any part of it. No paper benchmarks a maker-checker pipeline end-to-end against a single-session TDD agent at matched cost, so the experiment is not redundant.

## Implications for the design

1. **Confirm** the X/Y pairwise format and the one-Claude-one-GPT panel (§1).
2. **Change:** grade each pair in **both orders** with each Grader — 4 verdicts per issue.
3. **Change:** score each Grader by its **net across orders**, not by discarding order-inconsistent pairs. Strict "inconsistent ⇒ tie" would void roughly a third of ten pairs, which n=10 cannot afford. Publish the per-issue inconsistency rate as a diagnostic.
4. **Change:** when the two Graders' nets disagree in sign, record a **tie that stays in the data**. No third model, no Claude tie-breaker.
5. **Change:** make the **cross-run tests the primary measurement** and the Graders a secondary read.
6. **Change:** give each Grader the **full issue text** and a fixed hit-based rubric, not a holistic score.
7. **Change: normalise presentation, not just order** — strip commit metadata, branch names, PR text and agent chatter, and equalise formatting and comment density. Log diff size and test count so a length effect can be checked afterwards.
8. **Confirm, with its price named:** "≥8/10" is a 5.5% false-adoption rule, equal to the Bar at pass level 70% and margin **+0.20**. Report a near miss as **inconclusive, not "no better"**.
9. **Change:** score with the Bar's Dirichlet arithmetic so ties are zero-difference data; define void pairs **before grading**; reduce to **one difference per issue** before any test.
10. **Change: match the token and dollar budget across Arms**, or report the comparison as budget-confounded.
11. **Change:** spend extra budget on **more issues, not more repeats**, keeping a small repeat reserve for pairs that come out close. Pin every version; temperature 0 does not remove run-to-run variance.
12. **Confirm, with caveats:** test/implementation separation is the best-supported ingredient — but keep RED implementation-blind, keep test planning plain, and judge against a held-out oracle. The weakest are the **GPT cross-family reviewer** and the **five-round budget**. Instrument rounds (findings raised, accepted, rejected, and whether the suite's pass/fail state changed) so a win can be attributed and the follow-up can drop stages.
