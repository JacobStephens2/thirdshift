# Clear architecture diagrams: what the primary sources say

Research date: 2026-10-19.

**Question.** What do primary sources say about making technical and architecture diagrams that clearly communicate how a software system works to a newcomer? This is to inform a "clarity-first" variant of a web page that diagrams thirdshift — a Rust CLI that turns a GitHub issue into a pull request by running unattended agent sessions. The prototype then was `site/prototype-architecture/index.html`, three variants (A: paper reference figures; B: Mermaid; C: interactive swimlanes). It has since been discarded; the page it led to is [`site/architecture/`](../../site/architecture/index.html), built from variant A.

**Method.** Each claim is followed back to the party that owns it:

- **C4 model**: [c4model.com](https://c4model.com/), Simon Brown's own site, read live on 2026-10-19 — the [notation](https://c4model.com/diagrams/notation) page, the [diagram review checklist](https://c4model.com/diagrams/checklist), [abstractions](https://c4model.com/abstractions), [diagrams](https://c4model.com/diagrams), [system context](https://c4model.com/diagrams/system-context), [container](https://c4model.com/diagrams/container) and [dynamic](https://c4model.com/diagrams/dynamic).
- **Larkin & Simon (1987)**: the paper itself, read in full as a scanned PDF ([Cognitive Science 11, 65–99](https://mechanism.ucsd.edu/bill/teaching/F12/cs200/Readings/larkin.whyadiagramissometimesworth.1987.pdf), DOI [10.1016/S0364-0213(87)80026-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0364-0213%2887%2980026-5)).
- **Cognitive load theory**: Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, [*Cognitive Architecture and Instructional Design: 20 Years Later*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5), Educational Psychology Review 31, 261–292 (2019), read in full (open access).
- **Multimedia learning**: Mayer & Moreno, [*Nine Ways to Reduce Cognitive Load in Multimedia Learning*](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_6), Educational Psychologist 38(1), 43–52 (2003), read in full, including its effect-size table.
- **Physics of Notations**: the canonical paper is Moody, [*The "Physics" of Notations*](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSE.2009.67), IEEE TSE 35(6), 756–779 (2009), which is **closed access** (Unpaywall: `oa_status: closed`, no repository copy). I therefore read Moody's own restatement and application of all nine principles in Moody, Heymans & Matulevičius, [*Visual syntax does matter: improving the cognitive effectiveness of the i\* visual notation*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00766-010-0100-1), Requirements Engineering 15, 141–175 (2010), open access from Springer, with Moody as first author. All principle definitions quoted below are from that paper.
- **Progressive disclosure**: [Nielsen Norman Group's article by Jakob Nielsen](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/), accepted here as the originating source for the UI term.
- **Accessibility**: W3C WAI [Complex Images tutorial](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/complex/), and the WCAG 2.2 Understanding documents for [1.4.1 Use of Color](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html) and [1.4.11 Non-text Contrast](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html).

Scrollytelling mechanics. Animation. Anything about whether thirdshift's architecture is good; this is only about explaining it.

## TL;DR

- **Diagrams help because of *where things sit*, not because they hold more information.** Larkin & Simon's claim is explicitly computational: a diagram "can be better representations not because they contain more information, but because the indexing of this information can support extremely useful and efficient computational processes". And they are blunt that this is not automatic: "None of these points insure that an arbitrary diagram is worth 10,000 of any set of words. To be useful a diagram must be constructed to take advantage of these features." Their three named properties of a good diagram are **localization** (information used together is drawn together), **minimal labeling**, and **perceptual enhancement** ([Larkin & Simon 1987](https://mechanism.ucsd.edu/bill/teaching/F12/cs200/Readings/larkin.whyadiagramissometimesworth.1987.pdf), §5 and the free-body-diagram analysis, pp. 93–96). §2.
- **The single highest-leverage, cheapest, best-evidenced rules come from Mayer & Moreno's effect-size table** ([2003](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_6), Table 3): *weed out decorative material* (coherence effect, d = 0.90 over 5 experiments), *put the words next to the thing they describe* (spatial contiguity, d = 0.48, 1 experiment), *signal what to look at* (d = 0.74, 1 experiment), *break it into learner-controlled segments* (d = 1.36, 1 experiment), *name the parts before explaining the causal chain* (pretraining, d = 1.00, 3 experiments), *don't duplicate the same content in two places* (redundancy, d = 0.69, 3 experiments). Coherence is the only one of those with "strong evidence" in the authors' own words. §4.
- **C4 gives the only first-party, concrete diagram checklist in this whole literature**, and it is short enough to apply literally: every diagram needs a **title** (type + scope), a **key/legend**, named elements whose **type** is explicit, a short **description** per element, and **every single line labelled** with the intent of the relationship, matching its direction — Simon Brown's words are "Be specific and avoid vague labels like 'Uses'" ([c4model.com/diagrams/notation](https://c4model.com/diagrams/notation)). §1.
- **Hard numbers for "how much on one diagram":** Moody's own limits are **working memory ≈ 7 ± 2 symbol instances** before "a state of cognitive overload ensues and comprehension degrades rapidly" (complexity management) and **≈ 6 distinct symbol types** in the notation, because "the human ability to discriminate between perceptually distinct alternatives (span of absolute judgement) is around 6 categories" (graphic economy). He also reports that hierarchical structuring "can improve end user understanding by more than 50%" ([Moody et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00766-010-0100-1), §8, §12). §5.
- **Multi-diagram pages need explicit glue.** Moody's *cognitive integration* principle requires both **conceptual integration** (a "longshot diagram" — an overview that acts as "an overall cognitive map into which information from individual diagrams can be assembled") and **perceptual integration** (cues for navigating between diagrams). Diagram *type names* must not be confusable ([Moody et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00766-010-0100-1), §9). §5.
- **Text is not the enemy of a diagram; it is half of it.** Moody's *dual coding*: "using text and graphics together to convey information is more effective than using either on their own", but "text should never be used as the sole basis for distinguishing between symbols". §5. This lines up with WCAG 1.4.1 (colour alone must not carry meaning) and with the W3C requirement for a **two-part text alternative** for complex images, shown to everyone: "Make long descriptions available to everyone to reach a wider audience with your content" ([W3C WAI](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/complex/)). §9.
- **Progressive disclosure is the right mechanism for depth, and Nielsen names its two failure modes**: a bad split between initial and secondary (the first screen "can't contain confusing features or you'll slow down user performance") and an unclear or mislabelled path to the second level ([Nielsen](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)). §7.
- **The prototype's biggest clarity problems:** Figure 1 in variant A carries roughly 20 boxes, 4 nested zones and 15 arrows, of which **only 2 arrows are labelled** — a direct C4-checklist failure and a 2–3× breach of Moody's 7±2; variant B's flowchart has the same unlabelled-edge problem plus colour-only role encoding; variant C's lanes use colour as the sole encoding of actor type and hide every explanation behind a click, with no default-visible prose. Detail in §11.

## 1. The C4 model (Simon Brown)

### 1.1 Levels of abstraction

c4model.com's [abstractions](https://c4model.com/abstractions) page defines a hierarchy, in Brown's own words: "A software system is made up of one or more containers (applications and data stores)", each container contains "one or more components", which are "implemented by one or more code elements (classes, interfaces, objects, functions, etc)". People are "actors, roles, personas, named individuals, etc" who "use the software systems that we build". The four numbered levels are: 1 software system, 2 container, 3 component, 4 code.

The [diagrams](https://c4model.com/diagrams) page is explicit that you do not draw all four: include only the levels that add value, and "each zoom level can tell a different story to a different audience". The system-context and container diagrams are presented as enough for most teams.

Scope and audience, verbatim from the per-diagram pages:

| Diagram | Scope | Primary elements | Intended audience | Recommended? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [System context](https://c4model.com/diagrams/system-context) | "A single software system." | "The software system in scope." | "Everybody, both technical and non-technical people, inside and outside the software development team." | "Yes, a system context diagram is recommended for all software development teams." |
| [Container](https://c4model.com/diagrams/container) | A single software system | Containers within the system in scope | Technical people inside and outside the team: architects, developers, operations/support | Recommended for all teams |
| [Dynamic](https://c4model.com/diagrams/dynamic) | "A specific feature, story, or use case." | Your choice of software systems, containers or components | "Both technical and non-technical people, inside and outside the development team." | **Use sparingly** |

The container page adds a deliberate omission worth copying: it leaves out clustering, load balancers, replication and failover "because these vary by environment", and pushes them to one deployment diagram per environment.

### 1.2 Notation guidance

From [c4model.com/diagrams/notation](https://c4model.com/diagrams/notation). C4 is "notation independent", but the stated bar is that diagrams "should be understandable without a narrative".

- **Titles**: "Every diagram needs a title covering its type and scope, e.g., 'System Context diagram for My Software System.'"
- **Key/legend**: every diagram needs one, "explaining its notation, including shapes, colours, border styles, line types, and arrowheads". The page says this applies to UML, ArchiMate and SysML diagrams too — i.e. a legend is not optional just because the notation is standard.
- **Acronyms**: must be understandable to all audiences, or explained in the key.
- **Elements**: state each element's **type** explicitly (Person, Software System, Container, Component); give each element a **short description** of its responsibilities "for an at-a-glance view"; every container and component must have an **explicitly specified technology**.
- **Relationships**: "Every line represents a 'unidirectional relationship.'" Label **every** line, "matching its direction and intent. Be specific and avoid vague labels like 'Uses.'" Container-to-container relationships need a technology or protocol label.
- **Colours**: not prescribed. Keep colour coding consistent within and across diagrams, and "consider black-and-white printing and colour blindness".

### 1.3 The diagram review checklist

The [checklist](https://c4model.com/diagrams/checklist) is first-party and phrased as questions a reader should be able to answer. Verbatim, in full:

*General*: "Does the diagram have a title?" · "Do you understand what the diagram type is?" · "Do you understand what the diagram scope is?" · "Does the diagram have a key/legend?"

*Elements*: "Does every element have a name?" · "Do you understand the type of every element? (i.e. the level of abstraction; e.g. software system, container, etc)" · "Do you understand what every element does?" · "Where applicable, do you understand the technology choices associated with every element?" · "Do you understand the meaning of all acronyms and abbreviations used?" · "Do you understand the meaning of all colours used?" · "…all shapes used?" · "…all icons used?" · "…all border styles used? (e.g. solid, dashed, etc)" · "…all element sizes used? (e.g. small vs large boxes)"

*Relationships*: "Does every arrow have a label describing the intent of that relationship?" · "Does the description match the relationship direction?" · "Where applicable, do you understand the technology choices associated with every relationship? (e.g. protocols for inter-process communication)" · "Do you understand the meaning of all acronyms and abbreviations used?" · "…all colours used?" · "…all arrow heads used?" · "…all line styles used? (e.g. solid, dashed, etc)"

Note the two items most pages fail: **element size** and **border style** must mean something explained in the key, or must not vary.

### 1.4 Dynamic diagrams

From [c4model.com/diagrams/dynamic](https://c4model.com/diagrams/dynamic): a dynamic diagram shows how elements of the static model "work together at runtime to deliver a user story, use case, or feature". **Interactions are numbered to show their sequence**, and elements can be placed freely, with the numbers carrying the order. It is based on the UML communication (formerly collaboration) diagram; the sequence style presents the same information on a strict timeline, and you pick whichever you prefer. The guidance is to use them **sparingly**, for "interesting or recurring patterns or features that involve a complicated set of interactions" — not as a default diagram.

**Evidence strength for all of §1:** this is expert prescription from the model's author, not experiment. It is first-party and specific, which is why it is worth following literally, but nothing on c4model.com reports an empirical test.

## 2. Larkin & Simon (1987), "Why a diagram is (sometimes) worth ten thousand words"

Cognitive Science 11, 65–99. The abstract distinguishes **sentential** representations ("sequential, like a text", indexed by position in a list, each element adjacent only to the next) from **diagrammatic** ones ("indexed by location in a plane"). Two representations are **informationally equivalent** when each can be constructed from the other; they are **computationally equivalent** when, in addition, the inferences you can draw cheaply from one you can also draw cheaply from the other. Their thesis is that diagrams and sentences are routinely informationally equivalent but not computationally equivalent: "When two representations are informationally equivalent, their computational efficiency depends on the … operators that act on them."

From the conclusion (§5), the three reasons a diagram can beat prose, verbatim:

- "Diagrams can group together all information that is used together, thus avoiding large amounts of search for the elements needed to make a problem-solving inference."
- "Diagrams typically use location to group information about a single element, avoiding the need to match symbolic labels."
- "Diagrams automatically support a large number of perceptual inferences, which are extremely easy for humans."

And the caveats, verbatim, which are the most useful part for a design brief:

> "None of these points insure that an arbitrary diagram is worth 10,000 of any set of words. To be useful a diagram must be constructed to take advantage of these features. The possibility of placing several elements at the same or adjacent locations means that information needed for future inference can be grouped together. It does not ensure that a particular diagram does group such information together. Similarly, although every diagram supports some easy perceptual inferences, nothing ensures that these inferences must be useful in the problem-solving process. Failing to use these features is probably part of the reason why some diagrams seem not to help solvers, while others do provide significant help."

They also note the diagram only works "to those who know the appropriate computational processes for taking advantage of them" — i.e. a diagram presupposes a reader who knows how to read it, which is an argument for a legend and for prose that teaches the reading.

Their analysis of the physics free-body diagram (pp. 94–96) names three properties explicitly, and these read as design rules:

1. **Localization** — "Information used together is grouped together in the free-body diagrams." They point out novices violate this by putting a force in the wrong body's diagram.
2. **Minimal labeling** — "The only important functions of labels … is to relate parts of the real-world diagram unambiguously to the force diagram", and experts remove even that need by drawing the force diagram on top of the objects.
3. **Use of perceptual enhancement** — the skilled solver draws the right-directed force *larger*, so the reader can perceptually check consistency. That is: make a true property of the system readable off the geometry.

**Evidence strength:** theoretical and demonstrative (worked analyses in geometry and physics with a production-system model), not an experiment with human subjects. Enormously influential and the canonical citation; treat the three properties as well-argued design heuristics rather than measured effects. Note that "minimal labeling" is in tension with C4's "label every line" — see §10, rule 6, for how I reconcile them.

## 3. Cognitive load theory (Sweller)

Primary source read: Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, [*Cognitive Architecture and Instructional Design: 20 Years Later*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5), Educational Psychology Review 31, 261–292 (2019). This is the authors' own 20-year update of the 1998 paper and is open access.

The architecture: human cognition is "heavily constrained by our limited working memory which can only process a limited number" of novel elements; limits on working-memory capacity and duration "have been known at least since Miller" (1956); long-term memory is unlimited, and once information is in it, working-memory limits "disappear".

Three load categories, in their words:

- **Intrinsic**: "the complexity of the information being processed", determined by **element interactivity** — the number of elements that must be processed *simultaneously* because they interact. Crucially: "intrinsic cognitive load only can be changed by changing what needs to be learned" (or by increasing the learner's expertise).
- **Extraneous**: "not determined by the intrinsic complexity of the information" but by the instructional procedure; "it can be changed by changing instructional procedures". "Effective instructional procedures reduce element interactivity while ineffective ones increase element interactivity."
- **Germane**: working-memory resources "devoted to dealing with intrinsic cognitive load rather than extraneous cognitive load"; in the 2019 restatement it "redistributes working memory resources from extraneous activities to activities directly relevant to learning".

The effects that bear directly on diagrams, with the authors' own descriptions:

- **Split-attention effect** (Tarmizi & Sweller 1988): with a diagram plus statements that "are unintelligible for the learners until they have been integrated with the diagram", learners "must mentally integrate the two sources of information in order to understand … a process that yields a high cognitive load and hampers learning". The fix: "physically integrating the diagram and the solution statements, making mental integration superfluous". It applies to **temporal** as well as spatial separation. **This is the strongest single mandate for putting labels and explanations *inside or immediately beside* the figure rather than in a paragraph below it.**
- **Redundancy effect** (Chandler & Sweller 1991): when two sources are self-contained and say the same thing — their example is a diagram of blood flow plus text describing the same flow — "only presenting the diagram was superior to presenting both sources of information together", because learners spend effort discovering the two are identical. So: integrate *complementary* text; delete *duplicate* text.
- **Element interactivity effect**: "Cognitive load effects that are found for high element interactivity materials are typically not found for low element interactivity materials. Actually, cognitive load theory is only relevant for complex learning." A reminder that these rules matter most exactly where thirdshift is complicated.
- **Expertise reversal effect** (Kalyuga et al. 2003): "with increasing expertise, the above effects first decrease in size, then disappear, and can eventually reverse". Scaffolding built for a newcomer will actively harm an expert reader. Combined with the **guidance-fading effect**, this is the theoretical case for progressive disclosure: start with the overview and the hand-holding, let the reader fade it out.
- **Transient information effect**: information that "disappears after a few seconds, for example, in spoken text or in instructional video or animation" raises extraneous load; the compensations are "self-pacing or segmentation". Direct consequence: an animated or auto-advancing diagram is worse than a static one unless the reader controls the pace. Leahy & Sweller also found longer audio-visual pieces were *worse* than visual-only because of "the abundance of transient information".
- **Modality effect**: effective working memory can be increased by using visual and auditory channels together. Relevant only if the page ever narrates; for a silent web page, the fix for split attention is physical integration, not narration.

**Evidence strength:** strong. These are named effects with decades of replication and meta-analyses (the paper cites Ginns 2005 for a modality meta-analysis, Ayres & Sweller 2014 for split attention). The caveat the authors themselves put front and centre is that the effects hold for **novices** on **high element interactivity** material — which is precisely the newcomer-reading-thirdshift case.

## 4. Multimedia learning (Mayer)

Primary source read in full: Mayer & Moreno, [*Nine Ways to Reduce Cognitive Load in Multimedia Learning*](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_6), Educational Psychologist 38(1), 43–52 (2003).

Three assumptions, in their words: separate systems process pictorial and verbal material (**dual-channel**), "each channel is limited in the amount of material that can be processed at one time" (**limited-capacity**), and "meaningful learning involves cognitive processing including building connections between pictorial and verbal representations" (**active-processing**). All results they report are on **problem-solving transfer** tests, not recall — which is the right outcome measure for "can a newcomer reason about this system".

Their Table 3, reproduced with the authors' effect sizes (median *d*, number of experiments in brackets):

| Overload type | Method | Named effect | Effect size |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1: essential visual processing > visual capacity | **Off-loading**: move essential processing from visual to auditory channel | Modality effect | 1.17 (6) |
| 2: essential processing > capacity | **Segmenting**: "Allow time between successive bite-size segments" | Segmentation effect | 1.36 (1) |
| 2 | **Pretraining**: "Provide pretraining in names and characteristics of components" | Pretraining effect | 1.00 (3) |
| 3: extraneous material | **Weeding**: "Eliminate interesting but extraneous material" | Coherence effect | 0.90 (5) |
| 3 | **Signaling**: "Provide cues for how to process the material" | Signaling effect | 0.74 (1) |
| 4: confusing presentation | **Aligning**: "Place printed words near corresponding parts of graphics to reduce need for visual scanning" | Spatial contiguity effect | 0.48 (1) |
| 4 | **Eliminating redundancy**: avoid identical printed + spoken words | Redundancy effect | 0.69 (3) |
| 5: representational holding | **Synchronizing**: present narration and animation simultaneously | Temporal contiguity effect | 1.30 (8) |
| 5 | **Individualizing**: ensure learners can hold mental representations | Spatial ability effect | 1.13 (2) |

Details worth carrying into the design:

- **Segmenting** was operationalised as breaking a narrated animation into 16 segments, "each segment contained one or two sentences of narration and approximately 8 to 10 sec of animation", with the learner clicking CONTINUE. The authors flag the confound honestly: "Further research is needed to determine the separate effects of segmenting and interactivity." So: *reader-paced* steps, roughly a sentence or two each.
- **Pretraining** is a *sequencing* rule: "components are presented before a causal system is presented". Mayer's model is that building a mental model has two steps — component models, then the causal model — and pretraining lets the reader spend their capacity on the causal story. **For thirdshift this maps exactly onto a glossary-before-flow: name the Run, the Claim, the Worktree, the Harness, the Base branch first; only then explain the eight-step causal chain.**
- **Weeding**: the extraneous material in the experiments was "background music or adding short narrated video clips showing irrelevant material". Mayer & Moreno's own verdict: "The robustness of the coherence effect provides strong evidence for the viability of weeding" — the only place in the paper where they use the phrase "strong evidence".
- **Signaling** was a bundle: "(a) selecting words by stressing key words in speech, (b) selecting images by adding red and blue arrows to the animation, (c) organizing words by adding an outline and headings, and (d) organizing images by adding a map showing which of three parts of the lesson was being presented". That last one is a "you are here" map, and it is a signalling device, not navigation chrome.
- **Aligning**: the mechanism is eye movement — "learners tend to read a portion of text and then look at the corresponding portion of the graphic" (Hegarty & Just 1989). The prescription is literal: "placing the text within the graphic, next to the elements it is describing".

**Strength of evidence, by principle (Mayer & Moreno's own numbers):**

- **Strong** (many experiments, robust): temporal contiguity (8), modality (6), coherence/weeding (5) — and coherence is the one the authors themselves label strong evidence.
- **Moderate** (3 experiments): pretraining, redundancy, and spatial ability.
- **Thin here** (1 experiment in this paper): segmenting, signalling, spatial contiguity. All three have since accumulated much more support in Mayer's later reviews, but I could not read those (see §11), so I am reporting only what this paper establishes.

Mayer's later consolidation is [*Using multimedia for e-learning*](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12197), Journal of Computer Assisted Learning 33(5), 403–423 (2017), which reviews twelve principles. **I could not read it** — it is paywalled and Unpaywall reports no free copy. I cite it only for the record that the twelve-principle consolidation exists; every effect size above is from the 2003 paper, which I did read.

## 5. Moody, "The Physics of Notations"

The canonical paper is Moody, [*The "Physics" of Notations: Toward a Scientific Basis for Constructing Visual Notations in Software Engineering*](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSE.2009.67), IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering 35(6), 756–779 (2009). It is closed access with no repository copy. All quotations below are therefore from Moody's own restatement and worked application of the same nine principles in [Moody, Heymans & Matulevičius (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00766-010-0100-1), Requirements Engineering 15, 141–175, which I read in full.

His own one-line summaries of the nine, verbatim from §3.4 of that paper:

1. **Semiotic Clarity**: "there should be a 1:1 correspondence between semantic constructs and graphical symbols"
2. **Perceptual Discriminability**: "symbols should be clearly distinguishable from one another"
3. **Semantic Transparency**: "use symbols whose appearance suggests their meaning"
4. **Complexity Management**: "include explicit mechanisms for dealing with complexity"
5. **Cognitive Integration**: "include explicit mechanisms to support integration of information from different diagrams"
6. **Visual Expressiveness**: "use the full range and capacities of visual variables"
7. **Dual Coding**: "use text to complement graphics"
8. **Graphic Economy**: "keep the number of different graphical symbols cognitively manageable"
9. **Cognitive Fit**: "use different visual dialects for different tasks and/or audiences"

He is explicit that these are "desirable and measurable properties of notations", that improving any one increases cognitive effectiveness "subject to tradeoffs among them", and that the framework is a causal theory with the principles as independent variables and cognitive effectiveness as the sole dependent variable. He publishes an interaction matrix because the principles conflict.

The parts with teeth for a web page:

**Perceptual discriminability** is governed by **visual distance**: "the number of visual variables on which symbols differ and the size of these differences (measured by the number of perceptible steps)". "In general, the greater the visual distance between symbols, the faster and more accurately they will be recognised: if differences are too subtle, errors in interpretation can result." And the line that settles the newcomer question: "**Discriminability requirements are much higher for novices than for experts** as we are able to make much finer distinctions with practice." He also notes "Of all visual variables, **shape plays a privileged role** in perceptual discrimination, as it is the primary basis on which we classify objects in the real world."

**Complexity management** is about **diagrammatic complexity** — "the number of symbol instances or tokens on each diagram" — as distinct from graphic complexity. Two limits, in his words:

> "Perceptual limits: The ability to discriminate between diagram elements increases with diagram size. Cognitive limits: The number of diagram elements that can be comprehended at a time is limited by working memory capacity (believed to be seven, plus or minus two elements at a time). When this is exceeded, a state of cognitive overload ensues and comprehension degrades rapidly."

The remedy: "visual notations need to allow diagrams to be divided into perceptually and cognitively manageable 'chunks'. The most effective way of doing this is by **recursive decomposition**: allowing diagram elements to be defined by complete diagrams at the next level of abstraction." He reports that "Hierarchical structuring of RE diagrams in this way can improve end user understanding by more than 50%", and that "Complexity management is especially important for communicating with end users, who lack strategies for dealing with complexity". Note that this is the empirical backing for C4's zoom levels, from a different literature.

**Cognitive integration** applies "when multiple diagrams are used", and demands both:

- "**Conceptual integration**: to enable readers to assemble information from separate diagrams into a coherent mental representation of the system."
- "**Perceptual integration**: perceptual cues to simplify navigation and transitions between diagrams."

The named mechanism for conceptual integration is a "**longshot diagram**, a diagram that provides an overview of the system as a whole. This acts as an overall cognitive map into which information from individual diagrams can be assembled." He also flags that an overview which is itself too complex fails at the job, and — a small but sharp point — that confusable diagram *names* break integration: i*'s two diagram types are "too similar and easily confused", with "strategic" acting as "a 'noise word' as it lacks a precise meaning".

**Dual coding**: "According to dual coding theory, using text and graphics together to convey information is more effective than using either on their own. This suggests that textual encoding is most effective when it is used in a supporting role: to supplement rather than to substitute for graphics. In particular, **text should never be used as the sole basis for distinguishing between symbols**, but can be usefully used as a form of redundant coding, to reinforce and clarify meaning." On relationship labels specifically, he treats *unlabelled* relationships as a defect: "most relationships on i* diagrams don't have labels at all", and separately criticises using labels merely to tell relationship *types* apart as "an inappropriate use of labels". Note that his redundant-coding advice (encode the same distinction in two visual variables at once) is the Moody route to WCAG 1.4.1 compliance.

**Graphic economy**: "Graphic complexity refers to the number of different symbol types in a notation: the size of its visual vocabulary. Empirical studies show that graphic complexity significantly reduces understanding of RE diagrams, especially by novices. The reason is that the human ability to discriminate between perceptually distinct alternatives (span of absolute judgement) is around 6 categories: this defines an effective upper limit for graphic complexity." i* scores 16 and he calls that "an order of magnitude" over the limit. His four remedies: reduce semantic complexity, partition it across diagram types, increase visual expressiveness, or use symbol deficit deliberately.

**Cognitive fit**: from cognitive fit theory, "problem solving performance (≈ cognitive effectiveness) is determined by a three-way fit between the problem representation, task characteristics, and problem solver skills". His prescription for a novice audience is a direct checklist: "Notations designed for communication with novices should use easily discriminable symbols (Perceptual Discriminability), mnemonic conventions (Semantic Transparency), reduced complexity (Complexity Management), supporting text (Dual Coding) and a simplified visual vocabulary (Graphic Economy)."

**Evidence strength:** mixed, and Moody says so. The *principles* are synthesised from theory plus cited empirical work in perception and cognition, so each has a different pedigree: 7±2 and span-of-absolute-judgement rest on Miller (1956); the >50% hierarchical-structuring gain and the graphic-complexity finding are cited to specific empirical studies; semiotic clarity is largely analytic. Later work (e.g. van der Linden & Hadar, 2016) notes that "not all principles lend themselves equally to a clear and unambiguous operationalization". Treat the numeric limits as well-sourced rules of thumb and the principle set as the best available vocabulary for *arguing about* a notation.

## 6. Tufte

- *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, Edward R. Tufte's page calls it "The classic book on statistical graphics, charts, tables" and lists among its topics "**The data-ink ratio**", "**Design of the high-resolution displays, small multiples**", and "Detection of graphical deception: design variation vs. data variation". The page does **not** define the data-ink ratio, chartjunk, data density, or graphical excellence/integrity.
- *Envisioning Information*, Edward R. Tufte, 1990, 126 pages, Graphics Press ([publisher page](https://www.edwardtufte.com/book/envisioning-information/)). Tufte's own description: "This book celebrates escapes from the flatlands of both paper and computer screen", "showing superb displays of high-dimensional complex data." Listed topics: "escaping flatland, color and information, micro/macro designs, **layering and separation**, **small multiples**, and narratives." Again, topics only.
- *Beautiful Evidence*, Edward R. Tufte, 2006, 214 pages, Graphics Press ([publisher page](https://www.edwardtufte.com/book/beautiful-evidence/)). **What I am therefore not claiming.** I have *not* verified Tufte's definitions of the data-ink ratio ("the proportion of a graphic's ink devoted to the non-redundant display of data-information"), the "maximise the data-ink ratio" and "erase non-data-ink" injunctions, the definition of chartjunk, or the small-multiples argument, and I have not verified page numbers. Anyone citing those should cite the book and edition with a page number they have checked: *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, 2nd ed., Graphics Press, 2001 — the data-ink and chartjunk material is in the second part of the book ("Theory of Data Graphics"). **Do not cite a blog's paraphrase of Tufte as Tufte.**

**What survives for the brief anyway.** The three ideas that matter here are independently supported by sources I *did* read, so the page's design does not rest on unverified Tufte:

- *Remove decoration that carries no information* → Mayer & Moreno's coherence effect, d = 0.90 over 5 experiments, §4. This is the best-evidenced statement of the chartjunk intuition.
- *Layering and separation* (make the primary structure read first, push secondary detail into a quieter visual layer) → Moody's visual expressiveness and perceptual discriminability, §5; and a legitimate reading of Larkin & Simon's "perceptual enhancement", §2.
- *Small multiples* (repeat one constant frame and vary one thing, so differences are read by comparison, not by memory) → Moody's cognitive integration (consistent frames are perceptual integration cues) and Sweller's split-attention/element-interactivity reasoning: holding the previous diagram in memory to compare it with the next is exactly the mental integration you should make unnecessary. **For thirdshift this is the strongest argument for drawing the Run once and then showing Merge run / Spec run / Passes as the same figure with the delta highlighted — which is what prototype variant A already does with its `new` tag.**

**Evidence strength:** Tufte's principles are expert design argument with historical case analysis, not controlled experiment; and here I could verify only their existence, not their wording. Rank them below Mayer/Sweller when they conflict.

## 7. Progressive disclosure (Nielsen)

Source: Jakob Nielsen, [*Progressive Disclosure*](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/), Nielsen Norman Group. Accepted here as the originating source for the UI term.

Definition: defer "advanced or rarely used features to a secondary screen"; "Initially, show users only a few of the most important options", and "Disclose these secondary features only if a user asks for them."

Rationale, in Nielsen's terms, is that it "improves 3 of usability's 5 components: learnability, efficiency of use, and error rate":

- **Learnability**: novices can focus on the features most likely to help them, and "the very fact that something appears on the initial display tells users that it's important" — i.e. the first level is itself a signal about importance.
- **Error rate**: hiding advanced settings keeps novices from mistakes with options they don't need.
- **Efficiency of use**: experts aren't made to scan past things they rarely want.

Two things must be right, and both are failure modes to test for:

1. **The split.** Frequently needed features go first and the primary list stays short; "the initial display can't contain confusing features or you'll slow down user performance."
2. **The path between levels.** The mechanism for moving to the secondary level must be easy to find, and *its label must set accurate expectations about what lies beyond it*.

Nielsen does not use the phrase "cognitive load", though the reasoning is adjacent. The load-theoretic justification for progressive disclosure, and specifically for letting experts skip the scaffolding, is the **expertise reversal** and **guidance-fading** effects in §3.

**Evidence strength:** practitioner guidance from usability testing experience; no effect sizes given in the article. Converges with Mayer's segmenting (d = 1.36, 1 experiment) and with Sweller's expertise reversal, both better evidenced.

## 8. First-party guidance on sequence and flow diagrams

**C4's dynamic diagram** (see §1.4) is the first-party architecture-diagram answer: **number the interactions**, use them sparingly, and either a free-form collaboration layout or a sequence timeline will do. The UML lineage is the communication diagram.

**On the UML specification itself**: I did not read OMG's UML 2.5.1 spec for this note. It is worth being clear why, so nobody assumes it was skipped by accident: the UML spec defines the *semantics and syntax* of interaction diagrams, not their readability. There is no readability checklist in it to cite. If a claim about UML notation is needed later, cite the OMG spec directly ([formal/2017-12-05](https://www.omg.org/spec/UML/2.5.1/)) and read the clause.

**Mermaid** ([sequence-diagram docs](https://mermaid.js.org/syntax/sequenceDiagram.html)), first-party, and relevant because the prototype's variant B uses it:

- **`autonumber`** attaches a sequence number to each arrow — "It is possible to get a sequence number attached to each arrow" — and takes a start and increment. This is the mechanism for making diagram steps match numbered prose. The prototype already uses it.
- **Participant order is source order**: "The participants or actors are rendered in order of appearance in the diagram source text." To control the left-to-right order (and therefore whether arrows cross), declare participants explicitly up front. Crossing-minimisation is manual.
- **`actor` vs `participant`**: `actor` renders a stick figure instead of a labelled rectangle. That is the one built-in shape distinction between a human and a system, and it is the cheapest way to satisfy "consistent encoding of actor type" without relying on colour.
- **`box`** groups related participants into a labelled, optionally coloured vertical box — a grouping/legend device. Hex colours are not supported in `box`; use `rgb`/`rgba`/`hsl`/`hsla`.
- **`loop`, `alt`/`else`, `opt`, `par`, `critical`, `break`, `rect`** give labelled control-flow regions. The docs offer no readability guidance for them beyond the syntax.
- **Notes** attach with `right of` / `left of` / `over`, and can span two participants. Line breaks are allowed in notes and messages — relevant to keeping labels short enough to read.
- **Layout knobs** live in `mermaid.sequenceConfig`: `mirrorActors` (default false) repeats the actors below the diagram; `diagramMarginX/Y`, `messageMargin`, `noteMargin`, `boxTextMargin` control spacing; default font sizes are 14 for actors and notes, 16 for messages.
- **Gotcha**: avoid the bare word `end` in labels; wrap it in parentheses, quotes or brackets.

The docs give **no** guidance on how many participants or messages is too many — that limit has to come from Moody's 7±2 (§5).

## 9. Accessibility of diagrams

### 9.1 Complex images (W3C WAI)

Source: [W3C WAI Images Tutorial — Complex Images](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/complex/). "Complex images contain more information than a short phrase or sentence can convey", and the page names "graphs, charts, flow and organizational charts, detailed diagrams, and maps" — so every figure on this page is a complex image. For these, "a two-part text alternative is required":

1. A **short description** that identifies the image and says where the long description is.
2. A **long description** giving "a textual version of the image's essential information".

The guidance that matters most for a clarity-first design is that the long description should not be hidden in markup: "**Make long descriptions available to everyone to reach a wider audience with your content**", for example as part of the main content. The page also says to summarise complex images in the surrounding text, and — notably — "Where possible, reduce unnecessary complexity in the image itself." Accessibility and clarity point the same way here.

The techniques, as the tutorial presents them:

- **Approach 1**: a text link next to the image to a separate page or on-page section holding the long description; the link text should state the destination. Works everywhere, but the link is not semantically tied to the image. The semantic fix is to wrap the image and link in `<figure role="group">` with a `<figcaption>`.
- **Approach 2**: when the long description is on the same page, the `alt` names where it is (e.g. a heading); the location must be accurate.
- **Approach 3** (structural association): image and long description both inside `<figure role="group">`, the description in `<figcaption>`, where it may use headings, paragraphs and a data table; `alt` points to it.
- **`aria-describedby`**: links the image to a paragraph by `id`. The tutorial warns the referenced text "is read as one continuous paragraph, so headings and tables are lost. Use this only for text-only descriptions." This is a real limitation for a step-by-step description of a flow.
- A **data table** appears only as part of a structured long description inside the `figcaption` (Approach 3), not as a standalone alternative.

The tutorial "gives no specific advice on how to describe their structure or flow" for flowcharts — so the structure of the long description is a design decision, not a standard. The obvious shape for thirdshift is the numbered step list that already exists in README's *What a Run does*.

### 9.2 Colour and contrast (WCAG 2.2)

- **SC 1.4.1 Use of Color** (Level A): "Color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information, indicating an action, prompting a response, or distinguishing a visual element" ([Understanding 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)). The Understanding page's own example is a chart where "Mary's sales are in red, Tom's are in blue" — colour alone distinguishes the series, so a pattern, shape, label or text is needed. Its chemical-compound example pairs colour with a **number plus a legend**, so readers who can't tell the colours apart can use the numbers. Two further points matter: a lightness difference can count as the extra visual distinction **if it reaches 3:1**, but "if a reader must tell apart a specific hue (for example, green versus red), an additional visual indicator is still required"; and failure F13 applies when an image's text alternative omits information carried by colour.
- **SC 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast** (Level AA): "The visual presentation of the following have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against adjacent color(s)" — including **graphical objects**: "Parts of graphics required to understand the content", except where a particular presentation is essential ([Understanding 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)). The test is whether a person must perceive the graphic or part of it to understand the content. The threshold is strict: "the computed values should not be rounded (e.g. 2.999:1 would not meet the 3:1 threshold)." For a diagram this means **box strokes, arrows and arrowheads need 3:1 against their background**, not just the text inside them.

**Evidence strength:** these are normative standards (WCAG 2.2 is a W3C Recommendation) plus W3C's own non-normative guidance. Treat 1.4.1 and 1.4.11 as requirements, not advice.

## 10. The prioritized checklist for a clarity-first page

Ordered by expected clarity gain per unit of effort. Each rule names its owner and the strength of the evidence behind it.

### Tier 1 — do these first; they are cheap, and the evidence is strongest

1. **Start with one context-level picture of the whole system, and make it simple enough to serve as the map.** One figure, before any detail, showing the system in scope, the people who use it, and the systems it talks to. Sources: C4 system context — audience "Everybody, both technical and non-technical people", "recommended for all software development teams" ([c4model](https://c4model.com/diagrams/system-context)); Moody's **longshot diagram**, "an overall cognitive map into which information from individual diagrams can be assembled", with the explicit warning that an overview too complex to read fails the job ([Moody et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00766-010-0100-1) §9). *Evidence: first-party prescription (C4) + theory with cited empirical support (Moody).*
2. **One idea per diagram, and say what that idea is in the title.** Every figure gets a title naming its **type and scope**, in C4's own form ("System Context diagram for …"). Sources: C4 notation and checklist (titles, type, scope); Mayer's coherence effect, d = 0.90 (5) — anything not serving this diagram's one idea is extraneous; Moody complexity management. *Evidence: strong (coherence) + first-party prescription.*
3. **Label every arrow with a specific verb phrase, in the arrow's direction.** No bare lines, no "uses". C4's words: "Label every line, matching its direction and intent. Be specific and avoid vague labels like 'Uses.'" The checklist asks "Does every arrow have a label describing the intent of that relationship?" and "Does the description match the relationship direction?" Moody treats unlabelled relationships as a defect. *Evidence: first-party prescription, universal across sources.* **This is the single most mechanical, highest-yield fix available to the prototype.**
4. **Put every label, caption and annotation physically next to the thing it describes, inside the figure.** Mayer: "placing the text within the graphic, next to the elements it is describing" — spatial contiguity, d = 0.48 (1); Sweller's split-attention effect, with the fix being to physically integrate so that "mental integration [is] superfluous". *Evidence: strong for split attention (meta-analysed; Ayres & Sweller 2014), thinner for the single spatial-contiguity experiment; the two agree.*
5. **A key/legend on every diagram, covering every encoding that varies: colour, shape, border style, arrowhead, line style, icons — and box size.** C4: "Every diagram needs [a key/legend] explaining its notation, including shapes, colours, border styles, line types, and arrowheads", and it applies even to standard notations. The checklist's "element sizes" and "border styles" items are the ones usually missed. *Evidence: first-party prescription.* Corollary: **if a variation isn't in the key, remove the variation.**
6. **Keep labels short; let position carry what position can.** Larkin & Simon's **minimal labeling**: labels exist to tie the picture unambiguously to the thing, and experts remove the need by drawing on top of the object. Reconciling with rule 3: *relationships* get labels because the reader cannot infer the verb from geometry; *element identity and grouping* should be carried by position and containment rather than restated in text. *Evidence: well-argued heuristic, not experiment.*
7. **Never let colour alone carry a distinction.** Every role (human / deterministic tool / agent session / external system) must also differ in **shape or an explicit text tag**. Sources: WCAG SC 1.4.1 (normative), with the Understanding page's colour+number+legend pattern; Moody's redundant coding, and his "text should never be used as the sole basis for distinguishing between symbols" cuts the other way too; C4: "consider black-and-white printing and colour blindness". *Evidence: normative standard.*
8. **Cap the elements on any one diagram at about 7±2, and the distinct symbol types at about 6.** Moody: working-memory limit "believed to be seven, plus or minus two elements at a time. When this is exceeded, a state of cognitive overload ensues and comprehension degrades rapidly"; and "the span of absolute judgement … is around 6 categories: this defines an effective upper limit for graphic complexity". Both limits are tighter for novices. *Evidence: numeric limits sourced to Miller (1956) plus cited empirical studies; treat as a firm rule of thumb.*
9. **Split anything over the cap by recursive decomposition, not by shrinking the boxes.** One box on the overview becomes a whole diagram at the next level. Moody: recursive decomposition is "the most effective way" and "can improve end user understanding by more than 50%"; C4's four zoom levels are the same move, and C4 says to draw only the levels that add value. *Evidence: a cited empirical figure (>50%) plus first-party prescription.*
10. **Keep the full text explanation adjacent to and visible beside the figure — not hidden behind a click, and not only in `alt`.** W3C: complex images need a two-part alternative, and "Make long descriptions available to everyone to reach a wider audience with your content". Sweller's split attention and Mayer's spatial contiguity say the same for sighted readers. *Evidence: W3C guidance (non-normative but first-party) + strong CLT.*

### Tier 2 — strong evidence, more work

11. **Name the parts before explaining the flow.** A short glossary of the nouns (Run, Claim, Worktree, Harness, Base branch, Issue branch, Repair, Self-merge) *before* the first causal diagram. Mayer's **pretraining**, d = 1.00 (3 experiments): "components are presented before a causal system is presented", which lets the reader spend capacity on the causal model instead of on decoding names. *Evidence: moderate (3 experiments), directly on exactly this kind of "how a causal system works" material.* **thirdshift's CONTEXT.md is already this glossary; the page needs a 8–12-term subset of it, up front.**
12. **Number the steps, and make the numbers match the prose one-for-one.** C4's dynamic diagram numbers interactions; Mermaid's `autonumber` does it mechanically; README's *What a Run does* is already numbered 1–8. Make figure badge *n* = prose step *n* = `autonumber` *n*. Sources: C4 dynamic; Mayer's signalling, d = 0.74 (1), where part of the treatment was "organizing words by adding an outline and headings". *Evidence: first-party prescription + one experiment.*
13. **Segment the explanation into reader-paced, bite-size steps.** Mayer's segmentation effect, d = 1.36 (1 experiment), operationalised as 16 segments of one or two sentences each with a CONTINUE button. The authors flag that segmenting and interactivity were confounded. *Evidence: large effect but one experiment; converges with Sweller's transient-information remedies ("self-pacing or segmentation") and Nielsen's progressive disclosure.*
14. **Give the reader control of pace; never auto-advance or auto-animate the explanation.** Sweller's **transient information effect**: information that "disappears after a few seconds" raises extraneous load, and the compensations are self-pacing and segmentation. *Evidence: strong, named effect with replications.*
15. **Weed the figures: delete every mark that is not carrying information.** Grid backgrounds, decorative colour bars, gradients, glow/shadow text effects, ornamental rules. Mayer's coherence effect, d = 0.90 (5 experiments), and the authors' own "strong evidence" verdict; the extraneous material in those studies was decoration, not error. The Tufte chartjunk/data-ink framing says the same but I could not verify his wording (§6). *Evidence: strong.*
16. **Delete duplicate explanations; integrate complementary ones.** Two self-contained sources saying the same thing are worse than one: Sweller's redundancy effect (diagram-only beat diagram-plus-identical-text), and Mayer's redundancy effect, d = 0.69 (3). The rule is *complementary text goes inside the figure; duplicate text goes in the bin.* *Evidence: moderate-to-strong, and counter-intuitive, so worth enforcing.*
17. **Use small multiples for the variants: draw the Run once, then show each variant as the same frame with only the delta marked.** Merge run, Spec run and the Passes are all "the Run plus X". Keeping the frame constant means the reader compares by looking rather than by remembering. Sources: Moody's cognitive integration (consistent perceptual cues); Sweller's split attention/element interactivity; Tufte's small multiples (attribution verified, wording not — §6). *Evidence: inference from strong theory; the small-multiples literature itself I could not read first-hand.*
18. **Give the reader a "you are here" indicator across the set of diagrams.** Moody's **perceptual integration**: "perceptual cues to simplify navigation and transitions between diagrams". Mayer's signalling treatment explicitly included "a map showing which of three parts of the lesson was being presented", d = 0.74 (1). *Evidence: theory + one experiment.*
19. **Name the diagrams distinctly.** No two figure titles that differ by one word, and no noise words. Moody's critique of i*: the two diagram type names are "too similar and easily confused", with "strategic" acting as "a 'noise word' as it lacks a precise meaning". *Evidence: expert analysis.*
20. **State each element's type and what it does, right on the diagram.** C4: state the type explicitly (Person / Software System / Container / Component) and "Give each element a short description for an at-a-glance view of its responsibilities"; for containers and components, name the technology. For thirdshift the useful type set is *human · thirdshift (deterministic) · agent session · external system (GitHub) · artefact*. *Evidence: first-party prescription.* Note this interacts with rule 8: types cost symbol vocabulary, so cap the set at ~5.

### Tier 3 — correctness and polish, still non-negotiable where normative

21. **Every graphical object needed to understand the figure must hit 3:1 contrast against its background — strokes and arrowheads included, not just text.** WCAG SC 1.4.11, with no rounding: "2.999:1 would not meet the 3:1 threshold". *Evidence: normative standard.*
22. **Give each figure a two-part text alternative: a short `alt` identifying it and saying where the long description is, plus a structured long description in the visible `figcaption` (or a `<figure role="group">`).** Avoid `aria-describedby` for step-by-step descriptions, because the W3C tutorial warns the text "is read as one continuous paragraph, so headings and tables are lost". *Evidence: W3C first-party guidance; underlying SC 1.1.1 is normative.*
23. **Use progressive disclosure for depth, and get the two things Nielsen names right.** The first level stays short and contains nothing confusing ("the initial display can't contain confusing features or you'll slow down user performance"); the control to go deeper is easy to find and its **label accurately predicts what is behind it**. Pair with guidance-fading/expertise-reversal (§3): the scaffolding a newcomer needs harms the expert, so let it be skipped. *Evidence: practitioner guidance + strong CLT for the fading rationale.*
24. **Let shape, not just colour, distinguish humans from systems in any sequence diagram.** Mermaid's `actor` keyword renders a stick figure rather than a labelled rectangle; `box` groups participants into labelled regions; declare participants explicitly to control order and reduce crossings, since "participants or actors are rendered in order of appearance in the diagram source text". *Evidence: first-party docs (mechanism), with the rationale from rule 7.*
25. **Run the C4 diagram review checklist against every figure before shipping, as a literal checklist, with someone who does not know the system.** The whole of §1.3. The bar C4 sets is that a diagram "should be understandable without a narrative" — which is a stricter test than "understandable alongside our prose", and the right one for a page a newcomer lands on cold. *Evidence: first-party prescription; this is the only ready-made review instrument in the literature.*

Variant C is worst: the long description content exists (the `STEPS[].p` and `.li` text) but is reachable only one step at a time, by clicking.
- **Nothing numbered matches the prose.** Variant A's badges run 1–7 (stage boxes) but the bounds list is also 1–7 and the *workflow* figures restart at 1–4; variant B's `autonumber` runs 1–19; variant C's steps run 0–9 with the detail panel saying "step *i* of 9". Meanwhile README's *What a Run does* is numbered 1–8. Four different numbering schemes for one process. **Fix: one canonical numbering, README's, used in every figure, every badge and every prose step.**

### 11.2 Variant A (paper reference figures) specifically

- **Strongest of the three on the research, and the right base.** It is the only variant with per-figure captions, a figure-number scheme, visible prose adjacent to the figures, a legend, numbered badges, and — importantly — it already does **small multiples**: `flows.merge.draw()` calls `flows.run.draw(f, true)` and then marks only the additions with a lime `new` tag. That is exactly rule 17, and it is the one thing to preserve unchanged.
- **The hover-linked bounds list is a good signalling device wired the wrong way.** `.a-bounds [data-k]` ↔ `svg g[data-k]` cross-highlighting is Mayer's signalling plus Moody's perceptual integration. But it is **hover-only** (`onmouseenter`/`onmouseleave`), so it does not exist on touch and is not keyboard-reachable; and the list sits in `.a-col` (max-width 660px) *above* a 1040px-wide figure, so the two are often not both on screen — a split-attention cost the mechanism was meant to remove. **Fix: make the pairing click/focus-activatable, and place the list beside the figure.**
- **Figure 1 is trying to be the context diagram and the container diagram and the dynamic diagram at once.** It shows actors and externals (context), the inside of the binary and the worktree (container), *and* the seven ordered stages with step numbers (dynamic). C4 says each zoom level "can tell a different story to a different audience", and C4 says dynamic diagrams should be used sparingly. **Fix: split into (a) a context diagram, ~6 boxes: day shift, thirdshift, agent session, GitHub, OS scheduler; (b) a container-ish diagram of thirdshift + worktree; (c) one dynamic diagram with the eight numbered steps.**
- **Free-text notes are unanchored.** `.note(285, 536, ['unattended: full permissions of', 'your user, nobody approves'])` and `.note(860, 236, ['Fencing:', 'scheduled', 'and fixing'])` float in whitespace with no leader line to what they describe — split attention again, and Larkin & Simon's localization violated. **Fix: anchor every note with a leader, or move it into the element's own description lines.**
- **Hand-placed geometry is a correctness risk, not just a style one.** Label width is guessed with `textW = s.length * px`, and every coordinate is a literal (`M497 ${mid(3) - 72}V${mid(3)}H528`). Arrow endpoints are therefore not provably attached to box edges, and a text change silently mis-sizes a label background. For a diagram whose whole value is that "information used together is grouped together", geometry drift directly attacks the message.
- **Box size varies and means nothing**, which the C4 checklist asks about explicitly ("small vs large boxes"): the tab buttons grow 62 → 86 → 110 → 134px purely as decoration, and in the figures `Issue or Spec` is 92px tall while `CI checks` is 40px with no stated significance.

### 11.3 Variant B (Mermaid) specifically

- **Three diagrams of the same thing, with no stated difference in question.** The flowchart, the sequence diagram and the state diagram all describe a Run. Their ledes — "Who talks to whom", "The eight steps … as messages", "Every ending, and what becomes of the Claim" — do distinguish them, which is good, but the content overlaps heavily (the Repair loop and the Failed-run path appear in all three). Sweller's redundancy effect says two self-contained sources carrying the same information are worse than one. **Fix: either delete one, or make each answer a question the others cannot, and say so in the title.**
- **The sequence diagram's lede is wrong about itself**, which is a C4 "does the description match" failure at the page level: it promises "the eight steps of the README's *What a Run does*" and then renders 19 autonumbered messages. A newcomer counting will not find eight.
- **Layout is left to Mermaid, so crossing and ordering are unmanaged.** `flowchart TB` with 13 nodes across 3 subgraphs and `curve: 'basis'` will route edges wherever dagre puts them; Mermaid's docs are clear that participant order is source order and give no crossing guidance. Larkin & Simon's localization — "information used together is grouped together" — cannot be guaranteed by a layout engine. **If this variant is kept, it needs explicit participant declarations and probably explicit ranks.**
- **`min-width: 720px` with `overflow-x: auto` on `.k-panel`** means on a phone the diagram is a horizontally-scrolling strip: the reader can never see the whole figure at once, which defeats the single property (location-indexed, all-at-once) that makes a diagram worth drawing.
- **Third-party runtime dependency for the core content.** `import('https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/mermaid@11.4.1/...')` means the explanation is blank until a CDN responds, and permanently blank if it doesn't ("Mermaid failed: …"). The status line "Loading Mermaid from jsDelivr…" is itself extraneous text in Mayer's sense. Also worth noting against the repo's own stack research: [site-design-inspiration.md](site-design-inspiration.md) argues for static files served by Caddy.
- **The yellow note colour (`noteBkgColor: '#FFF200'` with `noteTextColor: '#0A0A0A'`) is the day-shift ink**, so "note" and "human" share an encoding. Semiotic clarity (1:1 construct-to-symbol) broken by accident.

### 11.4 Variant C (interactive swimlanes) specifically

- **The explanation is behind a click, and only one step's worth exists at a time.** `#c-detail` renders `STEPS[on]` only; nine tenths of the prose is hidden at any moment, and there is no "show all". This is the sharpest conflict with the research on the page: W3C says make long descriptions "available to everyone"; Sweller's split attention says don't make the reader hold the diagram in memory while fetching the text; and a reader cannot *read the page* — they can only interrogate it. **Fix: render all ten step descriptions as a visible numbered list beneath the lanes, with the click acting as signalling (scroll/highlight) rather than as the only access path.**
- **Lane membership is the primary information and it is encoded by vertical position plus colour only.** Who does what is the page's headline question ("Who does what, overnight."), and the answer is carried by row and hue. Add a text type tag per box.
- **The GitHub touchpoint pills say almost nothing.** `gh: 'reads'`, `'label'`, `'push'`, `'CI'`, `'merge'`, `'Issue'`, `'opens PR'` — single words on an unlabelled dashed line. Each is an arrow that needs a verb phrase and a direction (rule 3), and the dashed line's meaning is not in any key.
- **Step numbering is off by one and visibly so.** `STEPS` is 0-indexed, so the detail panel reads "step 0 of 9" for *Shape the work* and "step 9 of 9" for *Review*, while the box labels themselves read "1 Pre-flight" … "8 Clean up". A reader sees both schemes at once.
- **`color-mix(in srgb, var(--ink) 22%, var(--k))` for the selected step** is a ~22% tint against a near-black panel; selected vs unselected is a very small visual distance, and Moody is explicit that "if differences are too subtle, errors in interpretation can result" and that novices need *more* distance. Worth a contrast check against SC 1.4.11's 3:1.
- **Keyboard access is partial.** The steps have `tabindex="0"`, `role="button"` and Enter/Space handlers — good — but `.lanes .step:focus { outline: none }` removes the default focus ring and relies on `:focus-visible` changing only the rect fill, which is a subtle change (see above). And the variant switcher's arrow keys are local-only, which is fine for a prototype.
- **`min-width: 860px`** — same horizontal-scroll problem as variant B.

### 11.5 What a clarity-first variant should keep

Variant A's bones: paper-like figures with real captions, figure numbers, visible adjacent prose, a legend, numbered badges, the bounds↔figure cross-highlight, and above all the **small-multiples treatment of the four workflows**. Then: split Figure 1 into levels, label every arrow, add shape (not just colour) per role, add a glossary before the first figure, renumber everything to README's 1–8, put the long descriptions on the page in visible `figcaption`s, and weed the grid, colourbar and text-shadows.

## 12. What I could not verify, and what to do about it

- **Tufte's books.** I read only Tufte's own publisher pages, which name the topics but define nothing. Data-ink ratio, chartjunk, data density, graphical integrity, the layering-and-separation argument, the small-multiples argument and the principles of analytical design are all **unverified wording** here. See §6 for which of them are independently supported by sources I did read. If an exact Tufte quotation is needed, read the book and cite edition and page.
- **Moody (2009) IEEE TSE.** Closed access, no repository copy (Unpaywall: `oa_status: closed`). All nine principles are quoted from Moody's own 2010 Requirements Engineering paper instead, which restates and applies them; the definitions should be identical in substance but the 2009 page numbers are not available to me.
- **Mayer (2017), *Using multimedia for e-learning*, JCAL 33(5), 403–423.** Paywalled; no free copy per Unpaywall. The twelve-principle consolidation and its per-principle effect sizes are therefore **not** cited here. Everything numeric in §4 is from Mayer & Moreno (2003), which I read in full.
- **ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010** (architecture description; viewpoints framing stakeholder concerns) would have been a good standards-level source for "one diagram, one audience, one concern". iso.org returned HTTP 403 and iso-architecture.org refused the connection. Unread, uncited.
- **OMG UML 2.5.1 specification.** Deliberately not read: it defines interaction-diagram syntax and semantics, not readability, so there is nothing in it to cite for this question. C4's dynamic-diagram page carries the UML lineage (§1.4, §8).
- **The single-experiment effect sizes** in §4 (segmenting 1.36, signalling 0.74, spatial contiguity 0.48) rest on one study each *as reported in that 2003 paper*. All three are now much better supported in the wider literature, but I did not read that literature, so the checklist labels them honestly.
